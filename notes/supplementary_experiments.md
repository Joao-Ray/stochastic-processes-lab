# Supplementary experiments: design and interpretation

The [executed notebook](../notebooks/06_supplementary_validation.ipynb) contains three additional studies, following the same eight sections as the core notebooks. The [result record](supplementary_results.json) is generated by execution and includes configurations, every simulation seed, runtime versions, and numerical tables. The study design fixes the grids and replication counts before inspecting results.

## 1. OU discretization and sampling error

The exact OU update on a grid is

$$
X_{t+h}=\mu+e^{-\theta h}(X_t-\mu)
+\sigma\sqrt{\frac{1-e^{-2\theta h}}{2\theta}}Z,\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).
$$

The independent Gaussian noise term follows from the variance of the stochastic integral in the linear SDE solution. `simulate_ou_exact` implements this distribution; it does not reconstruct a continuous path between grid points.

Euler uses $a=1-\theta h$ and noise variance $\sigma^2h$. Starting from a deterministic state, its moments obey

$$m_{k+1}=\mu+a(m_k-\mu),\qquad v_{k+1}=a^2v_k+\sigma^2h.$$

Solving the second recurrence gives $v_k=\sigma^2h\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}a^{2j}$. If $|a|<1$, its stationary value is $\sigma^2/(2\theta-\theta^2h)$, not the SDE variance $\sigma^2/(2\theta)$.

**Design.** At $T=4$, hold $(\theta,\mu,\sigma,x_0)=(0.7,1.5,0.8,-1)$ fixed and compare six steps $h\in\{1,1/2,1/4,1/8,1/16,1/32\}$. Every method/step combination has three independent batches of 20,000 paths. Pooling their endpoints gives 60,000 independent Gaussian observations for each variance estimate. Exact and Euler simulations use separate streams.

**Recorded evidence.** The continuous variance is 0.455452. Euler's empirical variance is 0.699773 at $h=1$ and 0.461294 at $h=1/32$, corresponding to relative errors 53.64% and 1.28%. On the three finest steps, the slope of the **analytic finite-time variance bias** is 1.0236. This is not an estimate of pathwise strong convergence.

We display chi-square 95% intervals for each variance. Ten of twelve intervals contain their own method's predicted variance. Both intervals at $h=1/8$ miss: the exact solver's empirical variance is 0.462453, and its interval [0.457265, 0.467731] excludes 0.455452. This finite-sample miss is retained. Pointwise 95% coverage is not a guarantee that all intervals in a collection contain their targets.

**What this answers.** More paths reduce sampling uncertainty around Euler's discretized variance. A smaller step reduces its numerical bias. The exact solver supplies a benchmark with no discretization bias at grid points.

## 2. Markov mixing and periodicity

For $P_\alpha=((1-\alpha,\alpha),(\alpha,1-\alpha))$, write the starting law as

$$ (1,0)=(1/2,1/2)+(1/2,-1/2). $$

The second vector is multiplied by $1-2\alpha$ at each step, giving

$$d_{\mathrm{TV}}(\mathcal L(X_n),(1/2,1/2))=\tfrac12|1-2\alpha|^n.$$

**Design.** Use $\alpha=0.2,0.02,1$, both initial states, and 160 steps. Each combination has five independent batches of 10,000 paths. The empirical marginal law pools 50,000 paths, with pointwise Wilson intervals for the state-0 probability. Different times share paths, so their errors are correlated.

**Recorded evidence.** Both nonperiodic chains approach $(1/2,1/2)$ at their predicted rates. With $\alpha=0.02$, starting from 0 gives theoretical state-0 probability 0.500728 and empirical probability 0.498660 at step 160. A late-time empirical total variation distance fluctuates at the sampling scale even when the exact distance continues shrinking.

At $\alpha=1$, states alternate deterministically. The unique stationary distribution is still $(1/2,1/2)$, but a chain starting at one fixed state has total variation distance 0.5 at every step. Time-averaged occupancy tends to 1/2 because visits alternate; marginal convergence fails because the period is two.

**What this answers.** A stationary law is invariant if the chain starts in it. Convergence from another starting state needs additional conditions. Slow mixing also requires longer runs to distinguish a transient from sampling noise.

## 3. Control variates and interval calibration

For $U$ uniform on $[0,1]$, set $f(U)=e^{-U^2}$ and $g(U)=U^2$, with known mean $E[g]=1/3$. The adjusted variable

$$Y_\beta=f(U)-\beta(g(U)-1/3)$$

has the same expectation as $f(U)$ for a fixed coefficient. Expanding its variance and differentiating gives the optimal coefficient $\beta^*=\operatorname{Cov}(f,g)/\operatorname{Var}(g)$.

**Design.** Estimate the coefficient from an independent 5,000-point pilot and freeze it. For each $N\in\{100,300,1000,3000,10000\}$, simulate 1,000 independent evaluation rows. The plain and adjusted methods use the same points within each row. The known integral $\sqrt\pi\,\operatorname{erf}(1)/2$ is used only to calculate errors and coverage, never to train the coefficient.

The pilot coefficient is -0.666434. Numerical quadrature of the squared centered variables predicts a conditional single-draw variance ratio of 66.01. At $N=10,000$, empirical RMSE is 0.001925 for plain sampling and 0.000244 for the control variate. The squared-RMSE ratio is 62.27; it combines finite-sample bias and variance rather than being an exact empirical variance ratio. Fitted RMSE slopes are -0.5074 and -0.5038.

**Interval calibration.** Each row produces an interval $\widehat I\pm1.96\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}$. At $N=10,000$, coverage is 0.958 for plain MC and 0.947 for the control variate. Wilson 95% intervals for these coverage fractions are [0.944, 0.969] and [0.931, 0.959]. These describe uncertainty in the coverage assessment; they are different from a confidence interval for one integral estimate.

**Cost and scope.** The plots compare equal evaluation sample counts, excluding the pilot and extra arithmetic. They make no wall-clock or equal-total-cost claim. The pilot can be reused, but a one-off small-$N$ application must account for its overhead. Coverage is conditional on one pilot; assessing new pilots would require another replication level. The observed improvement is specific to this integrand and control. Both methods keep the $N^{-1/2}$ order.
